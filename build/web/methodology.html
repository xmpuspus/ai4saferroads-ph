Current vs Proposed Speed Limits: methodology

A transparent, open-data way to map, for every street, the current posted speed limit, the proposed safe limit, and why the two differ. It finds segments where the limit is higher than the speed a person can survive a crash at, given how the road is used. The Speed Safety Score below is the index that ranks them; it is about whether the limit itself is appropriate, not whether drivers exceed it.

1. Safe System recommended speed (Vsafe)

Each road segment is assigned a survivable speed from its function and surroundings, following the Safe System "survivable speeds" used by road-safety authorities.

ContextVsafeWhy
Pedestrians/cyclists mix with motor traffic (vulnerable-user site within 50 m, or a local road with frontage, or no sidewalk)30 km/hSurvivable pedestrian impact speed
Undivided arterial with side-impact intersections50 km/hSurvivable side impact
Divided carriageway / trunk highway (head-on only)70 km/hSurvivable head-on, no side impact. A one-way street alone is not treated as divided.
Motorway, grade-separated, no conflict100 km/hNo pedestrian or cross conflict
Living street / shared space20 km/hShared pedestrian space

Thresholds: Tingvall & Haworth (1999); ITF/OECD, Speed and Crash Risk (2018); WHO Speed Management Manual. Grade-separated roads (motorway, trunk) are never downgraded by nearby ground-level sites: an elevated expressway is not a pedestrian street.

2. Posted limit (Vposted)

Taken from the OpenStreetMap maxspeed tag. Where it is absent, a functional-class default is imputed and flagged as imputed. Official GPS-probe data (posted limits and operating speeds) replaces this step directly when available.

2b. Free-flow design speed (Vdesign)

The slow daytime pace comes from congestion rather than the road. To hold a speed that does not depend on traffic, each segment carries a second number, a free-flow design speed: the speed the built form invites when the road is clear. It is estimated from the functional class (a design-speed norm) and the alignment (curves cap it), so it is the same at 3am and at noon. This is a transparent class-based estimate, labelled as one, not a measured operating speed. On the flagged arterials it sits at or near the posted limit, so it does not add an independent claim there. Its value is holding a congestion-free speed for the roads that carry no posted sign, and framing the map's answer to "traffic never lets us reach the limit anyway". A measured lanes-and-width or GPS-probe layer would sharpen it.

3. The score

gap = Vposted − Vsafe
excess fatal-risk factor R = (Vposted / Vsafe)4  (Nilsson power model: fatal crashes scale with the 4th power of speed)
exposure E ∈ [0,1], a probabilistic OR of vulnerable-user sites within 150 m and WorldPop 2020 residential density
Speed Safety Score = 100 × min(gap, 40)/40 × (0.5 + 0.5·E)

Exposure blends how many pedestrian sites (schools, markets, transit) sit within 150 m with how many people live around the road (WorldPop 2020 constrained, about 90 m). Either signal can raise the weight. Because population density is continuous, the score spans the full range rather than landing on a handful of steps. Exposure can at most double a gap's score; it never creates risk where the gap is zero. The recommended limit is Vsafe, and the modeled fatal-risk reduction from adopting it is 1 − (Vsafe / Vposted)4, for example 60 to 30 km/h is about a 94% modeled reduction.

4. Data

Road network, functional class, posted limits, sidewalk tags, and vulnerable-user sites (schools, markets, hospitals, places of worship, transit stops) come from OpenStreetMap via the Overpass API. The full urban network of each city is pulled (tiled to avoid query limits), every street is scored, and the result is served as vector tiles (PMTiles) so the browser can draw the whole network smoothly. The same pipeline runs on any city; 51 Philippine cities are shown, from Metro Manila to regional centres across Luzon, the Visayas, and Mindanao.

5. Wealth overlay (correlation, not causation)

To ask whether the worst speed-limit mismatches fall on poorer or wealthier areas, the per-segment mismatches are aggregated to a roughly 1.6 km grid (the length-weighted mean posted-over-safe gap over segments that have a real posted limit; the gap is used, not the exposure-weighted score, so population entering the exposure weight cannot colour the wealth result), and each cell is matched to Meta's Relative Wealth Index: a model of relative wealth at about 2.4 km from the Data for Good program. The association is the Spearman rank correlation; spatial clustering is measured with Moran's I. Both carry a permutation p-value. The map draws a bivariate choropleth (mismatch tercile by wealth tercile). This is a correlation, not a cause: a relationship, or its absence, does not explain why, and spatial autocorrelation means nearby cells are not independent. The finding shown is whatever the data gives, not a chosen story.

5b. Crowding overlay (WorldPop)

The same grid supports a second reading: the mismatch against residential crowding, from WorldPop 2020. The flagged roads do sit in denser places (a median 14,620 people/km² around them against 9,326 for roads that already fit), but the size of the gap does not track density (Spearman about +0.01 across cells): crowding decides which roads flag, not how large the mismatch is. Taken to the national level the three pull apart. Metro Manila has the most flagged roads and the densest flagged network, yet the country's lowest road-death rate (3.6 per 100,000 against a national 10.9, PSA 2022). The deadliest regions are rural. The map screens dense city arterials; the deaths, recorded by region of residence, sit elsewhere.

6. Does it agree with real crashes?

The model applies a published physical law; it does not prove one. But its output can be tested against real crashes. We joined the 12,563 geolocated Metro Manila crashes the map shows (2018-2020, MMDA traffic alerts) to the scored network by snapping each to its nearest segment; 12,457 (99%) fall within 30 m of a scored road. The roads flagged here are 7.3% of the network length but carry 42.1% of those crashes, and crash density (per km, normalised for road length) rises with the Speed Safety Score across a 550 m grid (Spearman +0.41 over 1,222 cells). The flags land where crashes concentrate. This is an association on a convenience sample that over-reports arterials, with no traffic-volume control and mostly vehicle-to-vehicle crashes, so it is consistent with the model, not proof a limit causes a crash. A regional cross-check behaves differently and is expected to: PSA road-death rates are recorded by region of residence, not crash site, so Metro Manila has the most flagged roads yet the country's lowest death rate. The map screens at the segment level and validates there. Full numbers and caveats: see the project's validation notes.

6b. Is speed even the problem? Severity by hour

The objection is that the cities are gridlocked, so real speeds sit far below the limit. On the Mendeley EDSA crash set (22,072 records, 2007-2016, the one open PH set we found with both severity and timestamps), total crashes peak in the rush hour, tracking volume. But the share of crashes that injure or kill roughly doubles when the road clears: 13.5% in the late-night window (12am to 5am) against 6.7% in the rush-hour peak, about 2x (chi-square p = 1.3e-18, on a set holding 1,504 injury-or-worse crashes). Across all 24 hours the injury-or-kill share runs inverse to the crash count (Spearman −0.79). Two caveats keep this honest: the clear-road hours carry less enforcement and more impaired driving too, so this points to speed rather than proving it alone, and it is one road. The 22 fatal crashes in the set are too few to read hour by hour, so the test runs on injury-or-worse, not deaths. Independent of the hour, satellite imagery shows the flagged roads are wide, straight, multi-lane roads through dense housing (OpenStreetMap has no width tag, so the imagery supplies it), and only 21% of Manila's flagged roads have any traffic calming within 100 m (10% Cebu, 4% Davao). Congestion is temporary, the built form and the missing calming are not. Reproduce with build/overlay/emit_severity_artifact.py and build/overlay/satellite_evidence.py.

7. Limitations

  • OpenStreetMap posted-limit coverage varies by city (dense in Metro Manila, thin in smaller cities, and absent enough in a few that they carry no flagged roads at all). Imputed limits are flagged and excluded from the headline counts.
  • The Vsafe classifier is a transparent heuristic from open tags, not an engineering survey. Street-level imagery and official data would refine it.
  • Intersection density and divided-carriageway detection are not yet computed, so arterials default to the side-impact survivable speed (50 km/h) unless OpenStreetMap marks them as a divided carriageway. Official data would sharpen this.
  • Exposure blends point-of-interest proximity with WorldPop residential density; it counts residents and nearby sites, not measured daytime foot traffic or traffic volume.
  • This is a screening layer to prioritize segments for review, not a final determination.
Indicators are derived from open data (OpenStreetMap) and published road-safety research. A flag means a segment warrants review, not that any limit is wrong. Posted limits may have local context this screening does not capture. Not affiliated with ADB, WHO, or iRAP.
